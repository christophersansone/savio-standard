## 1. Executive Summary

Catholic grade schools promise a community formed by the Gospel, yet many families experience something different: social aggression that persists despite everyone's good intentions, a school office asked to carry burdens it was never built to carry, and a culture where the kindest families quietly leave. Left alone, a culture doesn't hold itself together — it drifts toward whatever its most persistent voices normalize, one grade, one group chat, one guest list at a time. The Savio Standard — a lay movement under the patronage of St. Dominic Savio, the schoolboy saint who organized his classmates to defend the smallest among them — exists to hold that culture on purpose instead. It is a parent-led response that does not ask the school or parish for permission, budget, or policy change. It organizes parents directly — to build real community, adopt a shared standard of conduct, learn a common language for social aggression, handle parent-to-parent conflict with charity and skill, raise children who stand up for one another through a dedicated children's program, and form children as wise, supervised users of technology from an early age.

The strategy borrows one proven mechanic from the Wait Until 8th movement — commitments organized by school and grade level that "activate" when a critical mass of families signs on, so no family stands alone — while charting its own course on digital formation. It draws its content from the best available research on relational aggression and bystander intervention, and its spiritual frame from the Church's own teaching that parents — not schools — are the primary educators of their children (CCC 2223; *Gravissimum Educationis*).

The antidote is not a single fix but a small set of durable artifacts, described in full below: a shared standard of conduct, a common language for naming what's actually happening, a scripted way to have the hard conversation, and a community structure that keeps every family known. How these get built, piloted, and rolled out — the year-one calendar, the content roadmap, the app, and how progress is measured — is the subject of the companion [Strategic Plan](strategic-plan.md).

## 2. The Problem We Are Naming

Every element of this case traces back to six observable realities that make up the drift. Naming them clearly — and charitably — is the first act of the Project, because vague language ("kids will be kids," "drama") is one of the mechanisms by which the problem persists. Nothing in this section is a criticism of any school or the people who serve in it; it is a clear-eyed account of why a community cannot delegate its culture.

1. **The Golden Rule has become a goal instead of a standard.** Parents enroll expecting their children to be surrounded by peers who treat one another with kindness and dignity. In practice, basic decency is treated as an aspiration to celebrate rather than a norm to expect.

2. **Parents outsource social conflict to the school.** We do this for understandable reasons — we trust the school and assume it is best positioned to act. But the arrangement is structurally lopsided. A parent has one child to advocate for and form; an administrator must balance the parents on both sides of every conflict, the teachers and staff, the pastor, the superintendent, the bishop, donor families, and privacy laws like FERPA that often prevent them from even explaining what was done. Meanwhile, the office is inundated with social conflicts that never required disciplinary action at all — ordinary friction that parents and children, with the right skills, could resolve themselves. Outsourcing overloads the school and atrophies the very muscles in parents — direct charitable conversation, community accountability — that actually resolve these situations.

3. **The school cannot enforce its way to a culture, especially in the upper grades.** Even the most dedicated administrator faces structural headwinds. Discipline alone rarely changes social behavior — relational aggression is precisely the kind of harm that slips past rules. Confidentiality obligations mean parents rarely see what was done, so diligent action can look like inaction. The most difficult cases often involve the families least receptive to feedback, and every disciplinary decision carries ripple effects across enrollment, staff, and parish relationships that parents never see. The conclusion is not that schools fail us — it is that a school office was never built to be the primary instrument of culture change, and expecting it to be one is a category error. The adults best positioned to shape the culture are not employed by the school.

4. **Parent-to-parent conversations fail.** When one parent approaches another about a conflict between their children, the default responses are denial ("there's no way my kid did that") or reversal ("your kid deserved it"). There is no shared standard, no shared language, and no neutral process, so these conversations damage relationships instead of repairing them — and drive parents back to the school office as the only channel left.

5. **Adverse selection hollows out the community.** Taken to its logical conclusion, the kind and loving families withdraw their children, concentrating the aggressive families that remain. Every kind family that leaves makes the school worse for the kind families still there. This is the death spiral the Project exists to reverse.

6. **Phones and social media are industrializing the damage.** Smartphones and group chats expose children to pornography, cruelty, and adult content years before they can process it, extend social aggression past the school day into every bedroom, and supply new weapons (screenshots, pile-ons, exclusion made visible). The research popularized by Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* links the phone-based childhood directly to the rise in anxiety and social cruelty this Project confronts — and no school policy can reach the family kitchen, the bedroom, or the weekend group chat where most of it happens.

None of this is unique to any one school, and none of it is fixed by better policy alone. A community of professed believers holds the two resources no administration can supply: the Gospel, and each other. The Project exists to put both to work — in partnership with the school's mission, on ground only parents can hold — reversing the drift on purpose, rather than waiting for it to reverse itself.

## 3. Theory of Change: Why Parents, and Why We Don't Wait for Permission

### 3.1 The theological warrant

The Church teaches that parents are the first and primary educators of their children — schools assist parents, not the reverse (Catechism of the Catholic Church 2223; Vatican II, *Gravissimum Educationis*). Organizing parents to form their children's character and protect their innocence is not a workaround of legitimate authority; it is the exercise of the most legitimate authority there is. This framing matters practically: it means the Project needs no one's approval, and it gives parents an answer when someone asks who authorized this.

### 3.2 Why culture change must come from parents

1. **Parents control the levers administration cannot touch.** Schools cannot control what happens at birthday parties, in group chats, at sports practice, or in the family kitchen where a child either learns contempt or learns charity. That is where culture is actually made.

2. **Bullying is sustained by the audience, not the bully.** Research on bystander dynamics consistently shows aggression persists because peers reward it with attention or silence. Reviews of bystander-intervention programs found nine of ten improved bystander confidence and intent, and most improved actual intervention behavior. You do not need to convert the aggressive families to change the culture — you need to convert the crowd. That is a numbers game parents can win.

3. **Critical mass beats policy.** Wait Until 8th grew from one Austin mom to 147,000+ pledged families at 58,000+ schools with zero institutional authority, because its pledge activates when ten families in the same grade at the same school sign — proof that visible, mutual commitment among a minority of parents changes what feels normal for everyone. The same mechanic applies to conduct, not just phones.

4. **Restorative, virtue-based approaches work in Catholic settings.** The Archdiocese of St. Louis's Virtue-Based Restorative Discipline reports that 86% of schools implementing with fidelity saw reductions in bullying. Its insight — form the adults first, treat mistreatment as a wound to be healed and a virtue to be built, not merely a rule broken — is the Project's pedagogical backbone, applied through parents instead of through administration.

### 3.3 The independence doctrine

The Project deliberately requires no approval from the school or parish. Institutions will likely resist at first, because parent organization reduces their control of the narrative. The posture is therefore: independent, never adversarial. The Project never publicly criticizes the school, never organizes against administrators, and always leaves the door open. The goal is that within two to three years the school asks to adopt the Project's language and programs because its families already speak them. Independence is also the growth strategy: a model that depends on an enthusiastic principal cannot scale — the model must work anywhere, whatever the local disposition, and be a gift to the school rather than a demand on it.

## 4. Vision, Mission, and Principles

**Vision.** Catholic school communities where the Golden Rule is the standard of daily life — kept by every family, not only the ones who signed onto it — where kind families stay, aggressive behavior finds no audience, and children's innocence is guarded by a united front of parents.

**Mission.** To equip and organize Catholic school parents — independent of school administration — to build real community, adopt shared standards, master a common language for social aggression, resolve conflicts between families with charity and skill, and raise upstanders.

### Patronage and Identity

The Project is a lay movement of Catholic school families under the patronage of St. Dominic Savio, and of his teacher, St. John Bosco. Savio is the Project's primary patron because he is exactly what this movement asks children to become: a schoolboy — fourteen when he died, canonized precisely for the holiness of an ordinary school life — who organized his classmates into a company of mutual care, defended the smallest boys from bullying, broke up fights, and befriended the friendless. Sanctity within reach of a schoolchild, expressed as courage on behalf of others: every skit, meeting, and covenant commitment this Project builds is an attempt to make Savio's way ordinary again.

Behind the pupil stands the teacher. Bosco's Oratory in the Valdocco district of Turin — part playground, part school, part church, part home — formed young people whom the institutions of his day could not reach, and his "preventive system" is the Project's method in miniature: form young people through reason, religion, and loving-kindness so thoroughly that discipline is rarely needed, rather than punishing failures after the fact. His axiom, "It is not enough to love the young; they must know that they are loved," is the standard for every meeting, conversation, and intervention.

**On the name.** "The Savio Standard" is the Project's name. The children's program will carry its own kid-facing name, still to be chosen (one candidate already in early development: a club built on the Golden Rule).

### Principles

1. **The standard, not the goal.** Kindness and dignity are the floor. We do not celebrate the absence of cruelty; we expect it.

2. **My child is capable of both.** Every family signs up to the premise that their child can be the victim, the aggressor, or the silent crowd — often all three in the same year. This single admission disarms the "not my kid" reflex that kills parent-to-parent conversations.

3. **Charity with a spine.** We assume goodwill, speak privately and directly, and never gossip — and we do not confuse charity with conflict avoidance. Naming harm clearly is an act of love toward both children.

4. **Restoration over prosecution.** The purpose of every intervention is a repaired relationship and a changed habit, not a verdict. This is both the Gospel and what the evidence says works.

5. **Independent, never adversarial.** We need no permission and we make no enemies. We speak well of the school or not at all.

6. **Together or not at all.** Commitments activate in groups, never alone. No family should be the only one holding a line.

7. **Confidentiality is sacred.** The Project is structurally protected from becoming a gossip network. What is shared in a facilitated conversation stays there. Children are never named in any written or digital record.

### Guardrails

Standing guardrails, adopted at founding and printed in every manual, that keep the Principles above from eroding under pressure: children are never named in written or digital records; facilitators never adjudicate and never carry information out of the room; the Project never publicly criticizes the school or parish; no Project channel may be used to discuss a specific family's situation; no Project platform carries messaging or user-generated content — the Project will not build for parents the very medium it trains children to resist; safeguarding escalations (Section 5, Pillar 4) override every confidentiality norm; and leadership roles rotate on fixed terms, so no ministry depends on one irreplaceable person.

### A public expression: The Convictions

These principles find their public, signable form in two companion documents: [The Parents' Convictions](../content/convictions-parents.md), a one-page statement of what Catholic school parents hold self-evident and expect of themselves and their children, and [The Community Convictions](../content/convictions-community.md), the same truths restated as shared affirmations for parents, teachers, staff, and school leadership to sign together. Where the Principles above govern how the Project itself operates, the Convictions are what a family — or eventually a whole school community — actually signs. A community cannot hold a standard it has never said out loud; the Strategic Plan tracks when each is finalized, but this is why they exist at all.

## 5. Program Architecture: The Six Pillars

Everything the Project builds — content, events, and eventually the app — hangs on six pillars. Each pillar answers one of the six named problems; each is described here in terms of *why* it is the right answer. The companion Strategic Plan covers *when* and *how* each is built and staffed.

| Pillar | What it is | Problem it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Community | School Leads and Homeroom Hosts — named volunteer roles serving the whole school and every homeroom; the relational fabric that makes everything else possible | Isolation; adverse selection of kind families |
| 2. Formation | Parent education series on social aggression, child development, and digital childhood | Outsourcing to an overloaded school office |
| 3. Shared Language & Standards | The Family Covenant plus a common vocabulary taught to parents and children alike | No shared standard; "kids will be kids" vagueness |
| 4. Conflict Navigation | A parent-to-parent protocol and trained volunteer facilitators | Failed parent conversations; denial and script-flipping |
| 5. Upstander Kids | The children's program (name TBD): monthly club meetings with skits and education, mentoring, and a companion learning app | Aggression sustained by silent bystanders |
| 6. Digital Oversight | Shared, non-technical household norms — message review, no bedroom devices, age-appropriate guardrails — plus pointers to recommended tools | Phones and social media crushing innocence |

### Pillar 1 — Community

Culture cannot be built among strangers, and a parent will not receive hard feedback from someone they have never shared a meal with. Two facts of Catholic grade school life make community unusually buildable — and unusually neglected. First, the grade: largely the same families travel together for nine years, the majority of their children's formative life. Relationships do form through sports and other extracurriculars, but those self-select, drift cliquey, and always leave families on the outskirts. Second, the homeroom: the room a child is placed in is effectively their entire social world for the school year, and rooms reshuffle annually — so by eighth grade nearly every family will have shared a homeroom with nearly every other. The Project builds on these two rhythms rather than inventing a new one, through two named volunteer roles: **School Leads**, a small school-wide team, and **Homeroom Hosts**, two to three families per homeroom, re-formed each year. Full role definitions, duties, and terms live in [Roles & Responsibilities](../content/roles-and-responsibilities.md); when and how each is staffed for the pilot is in the Strategic Plan.

The critical design rule: **roles are bounded; membership never is.** There is no group to join and no roster to be left off. Every gathering is open to the entire homeroom or school, invitations always go to all, and the named roles exist to serve the whole unit — a Lead or Host who convenes an exclusive subset has misunderstood the job, and the written role descriptions say so. This is the structural answer to the clique problem: structure lives in the roles, openness lives in the events. Inclusion is not a committee; it is the first duty written into every role.

### Pillar 2 — Formation

Parents cannot enforce a standard they cannot articulate, and most parents have never been taught the mechanics of relational aggression, the difference between conflict and bullying, or what actually changes a child's behavior. The formation program is an annual session series plus an evergreen content library, drawing on the relational-aggression literature (Simmons, Wiseman, Crick), bystander research, Virtue-Based Restorative Discipline, and *The Anxious Generation* — all framed in Catholic anthropology: virtue, conscience, the dignity of the person, sin and mercy. The session calendar and content library are in the Strategic Plan.

### Pillar 3 — Shared Language & Standards

Shared language is what converts "drama" — a word that hides everything — into specific, discussable behavior. This pillar rests on two documents, taught to parents and children alike so that a fourth grader, her parents, and the parents of the girl who excluded her are all using the same words with the same meanings.

**The Family Covenant** is the standard: one page, signed as a household, renewed each school year. Its eight commitments in brief: the Golden Rule as standard, not aspiration; humility (my child is capable of harm, and I will listen and thank the parent who tells me); directness (concerns go privately to the family, never to the rumor mill); teaching children to include and stand up; accountability and repair at home; digital oversight; presence at community gatherings; and prayer for the school's children. Like the Wait Until 8th pledge, it activates by grade: when eight families in a grade sign, all signers are notified and introduced to one another; until then, signatures remain private, so no family ever stands alone. The covenant deliberately contains nothing the school could object to, nothing that names or targets anyone, and nothing enforceable by anything except conscience and community — which is the point. Signing is a signal, not a shield: the Golden Rule it names already applies to every family, signed or not; what signing adds is a mutual, in-advance promise about how signers themselves will respond when another parent comes to them. Its second commitment is the heart of the document; it is the pre-commitment that makes the Parent-to-Parent Protocol (Pillar 4) work. Full text: [content/covenant.md](../content/covenant.md).

**The Glossary** is the language: the working vocabulary covering conflict vs. bullying, relational aggression, social exclusion, bystander/upstander, flipping the script, teasing vs. taunting, digital pile-on, and repair. It is taught to parents in the formation series and to children in the children's program meetings, with child versions by age tier (K–2, 3–5, 6–8) to follow. Full text: [content/glossary.md](../content/glossary.md).

### Pillar 4 — Conflict Navigation

This is the Project's most distinctive offering, and its answer to the failed conversation named in Section 2: a defined protocol for parent-to-parent conversations, and a bench of trained volunteer facilitators — respected parents, ideally with counseling, HR, mediation, or pastoral backgrounds — who can host a structured two-family conversation when direct contact fails. This replaces the current binary of "awkward confrontation in the parking lot" or "email the principal and hope."

The protocol has three modes. **Approaching** (when your child was harmed): pray first, get your child's account twice and hold it loosely, then contact the other family privately — opening with alliance ("I need your help with something between our kids, and I'd want you to come to me if it were reversed"), describing behavior rather than character, and closing with a small concrete ask and a follow-up date. **Receiving** (when your child caused harm): thank the parent for coming to you instead of around you — the sentence that is the culture change — listen without adjudicating on the spot, talk with your child, follow up when you said you would, and if your child did the harm, require repair, apply consequences at home, and close the loop. **Facilitated conversations**: when direct contact fails, either family may request a trained volunteer facilitator for a structured two-family meeting — restorative ground rules, no verdicts, written agreements, a follow-up date; a parent-level adaptation of Virtue-Based Restorative Discipline's circle practice. Facilitators receive training and a written scope: they facilitate, never adjudicate, and carry nothing out of the room. Full guide, with scripts and worked examples: [content/parent-to-parent-protocol.md](../content/parent-to-parent-protocol.md); facilitator training and the bench: [content/facilitator-onboarding.md](../content/facilitator-onboarding.md).

**Escalation boundaries.** The protocol handles social aggression between children of willing families. It does not replace mandatory channels: physical abuse, sexual matter, threats of self-harm or violence, or criminal behavior go to the school, the diocese's safe-environment office, and where applicable law enforcement — immediately and without a facilitated preamble. Facilitators are trained on these lines, and the written materials state them plainly. The Project also never counsels a family against reporting anything to the school; it adds a parallel channel, it never blocks one.

### Pillar 5 — Upstander Kids: The Children's Program

Children ultimately police their own culture, and the Project's children's program — name to be chosen by the founding team, with St. Dominic Savio as patron — trains them to do it. It is the kid-facing version of the whole movement: club meetings built around skits, stories, games, and short lessons that teach the shared language, the difference between conflict and bullying, and practiced upstander responses, run in age tiers from early elementary through middle school.

Skits are the signature method. Children rehearse the exact moments — the lunch table that closes, the group chat that turns, the "you can't sit with us" — until the upstander response is muscle memory rather than a poster on a wall, and older tiers performing for younger ones become teachers of the standard. A mentor program (Savio's own model: he did precisely this at his school) pairs trained seventh- and eighth-graders with younger grades, converting the top of the school's social hierarchy into its guardians. A learning-management-style companion app, already in early development, delivers the curriculum, tracks milestones and badges between meetings, and gives families a shared track to follow at home (see The App, in the Strategic Plan).

### Pillar 6 — Digital Oversight

The Project rejects both failure modes: unrestricted early access, and total prohibition that ends with handing a thirteen-year-old an unfiltered phone and no training. The middle path is an apprenticeship — begun early, with age-appropriate guardrails that loosen as responsibility is demonstrated. But the Project's teaching keeps its emphasis where parents have the most leverage and need the least technical skill: oversight. The covenant norms are simple, non-technical, and enforceable by any household: parents may review their child's messages at any time, and the child knows it; devices never sleep in bedrooms; access is age-appropriate and filtered; and no child receives unfiltered, unsupervised access at any age. Families who prefer a fuller delay (such as the Wait Until 8th pledge) are welcome and fully compatible — the covenant imposes norms, not a timeline. Adopted grade-wide, these norms dissolve both the "everyone else has one" pressure and the more corrosive "everyone else's is unfiltered."

The technical enforcement layer (device controls, filtering, staged profiles) is deliberately out of scope for the Project's own content: it is deep, fast-moving, and better served by dedicated products that can keep pace with the platforms. The Project's job is to make the norms normal; a tool's job is to make them easy — the founding team anticipates recommending a companion product, separately built and always disclosed as such, in the Strategic Plan's content roadmap.

## 6. Risks, Objections, and Responses

**"This is a clique of parents judging everyone else."** Signing is a signal, not a shield. The standard itself — the Golden Rule — judges no one new; it's what a Catholic school already asks of every child, said plainly instead of left vague, and it applies whether a family signs or not. What the covenant adds binds only its signers: a private, in-advance promise about how *they* will respond to each other. Its second commitment is an admission of one's own child's capacity for harm. Structurally, roles are bounded but membership never is: there is no group to join, every gathering is open to the entire homeroom or grade, and invitations always go to all (Pillar 1). Recruitment leads with humility, never grievance.

**It becomes a gossip network.** The single most likely failure mode. Countered structurally: confidentiality norms in the covenant, no forums in the app, no case records anywhere, facilitator discretion rules, and a founding-team culture of shutting down speculation about specific families on the spot.

**Administration reacts with hostility.** Expected initially. The Project gives them nothing to object to: off-campus, opt-in, never critical of the school, and its visible outputs (kinder kids, calmer parents, fewer conflicts landing on the office desk, healthier digital habits) make their jobs easier. The courtesy briefing to the pastor frames it as parents doing their catechetical job. If hostility persists, it changes nothing — that independence is the design.

**The aggressive families never join.** They don't need to. The strategy targets the audience, not the aggressor: when a critical mass of families share language and standards and their children are trained upstanders, aggression loses its payoff. Some aggressive families will join late, when the covenant becomes the school's visible social norm; some never will.

**A facilitated conversation goes badly wrong.** Facilitators are trained, operate in pairs for hot cases, refuse cases beyond their scope, and never handle safeguarding matters. A written scope document protects both the facilitator and the Project.

**Legal exposure.** One bright line: the organization publishes a framework; independent local parent groups run their own communities. Terms of use, indemnification, licensing, entity structure, and safety guidance are specified in [Stewardship & Operations](stewardship-and-operations.md).

**Founder burnout / hero dependence.** Defined volunteer roles, fixed-term rotation, everything documented for handoff, and content written once for reuse. Success is measured in part by whether the model could run without its founder.

**Parents sign and do nothing.** Activation mechanics (nothing activates alone), homeroom and grade gatherings that meet in person, and the formation series keep the covenant a live practice rather than a signed PDF. Annual renewal makes commitment a recurring choice.

## 7. Evidence Base and Source Material

Key sources informing this case, for the content team and for credibility with skeptical parents:

- **Wait Until 8th** (waituntil8th.org) — 147,000+ families, 58,000+ schools, parent-to-parent spread without institutional authority. The Project borrows its pledge-activation mechanic, not its digital policy; families who take the pledge remain fully compatible with the covenant.
- **Jonathan Haidt, *The Anxious Generation*** (2024) and anxiousgeneration.com — the research case linking phone-based childhood to the mental-health and social-aggression surge; by 2026, nine states restricting school phone use. The Project shares the diagnosis while taking an oversight-first rather than blanket-delay approach (Pillar 6).
- **Lynne Lang, *Virtue-Based Restorative Discipline*** (Archdiocese of St. Louis) — Catholic restorative model; 86% of fidelity schools reported reduced bullying; source for the facilitation method and "form the adults first" principle.
- **Rachel Simmons, *Odd Girl Out*** (2002, rev. 2011) and **Rosalind Wiseman, *Queen Bees and Wannabes*** (2003) — the relational-aggression canon for the formation series; underlying research by Nicki Crick.
- **Bystander-intervention research** — reviews of school-based programs (2007–2018) found 9 of 10 improved bystander efficacy/intent and 7 of 9 improved actual behavior; grounds Pillar 5.
- **Catechism of the Catholic Church 2221–2231; Vatican II, *Gravissimum Educationis*** — parents as primary educators; the theological warrant for independence.
- **St. Dominic Savio** — the Project's primary patron; the schoolboy saint who organized his peers to defend the youngest boys — and **St. John Bosco**, his teacher, whose preventive system (reason, religion, loving-kindness) and Oratory model supply the Project's method.
- **Openlight Media, Disciple of Christ — Education in Virtue** — Dominican virtue curriculum with a whole-family component; a model (and possible licensed content source) for the virtue vocabulary.
